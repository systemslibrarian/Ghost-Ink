The same trick, other disguises

Everything above hides data in characters. These five hide it somewhere else entirely — in the order characters display, in what a renderer chooses not to show, in what your clipboard receives, in what a string becomes after it is normalised, and in the low bit of an image. Same shape of failure every time: two readers of one artefact disagree, and only one of them is you.

The message is in which word was chosen

Every other carrier on this page hides bytes in characters you cannot see. This one hides them in vocabulary. A codebook groups interchangeable words — big / large / sizable / substantial — into ordered sets of two or four, and the rank of the word that actually appears carries one or two bits. Nothing is invisible, nothing is unusual, and the visible text genuinely changes. That is the trade: the X‑ray above cannot see this at all, but a careful reader might notice the prose has been rewritten.
The cost is severe and it is the honest lesson here. A payload needs the whole container, so a two‑character secret needs 184 bits — and this cover of nearly seven hundred words carries only a few hundred. Linguistic stego buys total invisibility with terrible capacity.

What just happened

Unicode reserves a block called Tags from U+E0000 to U+E007F. Almost all of it mirrors plain ASCII: U+E0041 stands in for “A”, U+E0061 for “a”, U+E0020 for a space. The characters exist in the text and travel with it, but fonts render them to nothing, so a person sees only the cover text.

  1. Your secret is turned into bytes, then Base64 so every character lands in the printable ASCII range.
  2. Each of those characters is shifted up by 0xE0000 into the Tags block — same letter, now invisible.
  3. The invisible run is appended to (or scattered through) the cover text. Position doesn’t matter: a reader collects the tag characters in order and shifts them back down.
Container format. Before Base64, the payload is one version byte, one flags byte (plaintext or AES-GCM), then either the UTF-8 message or salt(16) · iv(12) · ciphertext. With encryption on, the key comes from your passphrase via PBKDF2-SHA256 (210,000 iterations) and the message is sealed with AES-256-GCM in your browser — no bytes leave the page.

How attackers use this in the wild

This is not a party trick. The exact carrier this page demonstrates — the Unicode Tags block — is a live technique known as ASCII smuggling. It has had two lives, and a user’s suspicions are never raised in either one.

Life one · against AI

Smuggling instructions into a model

Malicious instructions are written in invisible tags and pasted into an email, a web page, or a document an LLM will process. The model reads the hidden commands as ordinary text; the human reviewing the same content sees nothing. This is a stealthy form of prompt injection, and it’s where the technique first drew attention.

Life two · against filters

Obfuscating keywords from a detector

Spammers slip invisible tags inside trigger words so a filter no longer recognizes them, while the recipient reads the word normally. Same mechanism, inverted goal: instead of adding a hidden instruction, they’re hiding a visible keyword from the machine that scores it.

In early 2026, Microsoft watched the second use explode. Hits on its ASCII-smuggling hunting signature in Microsoft Defender for Office 365 rose from roughly 21,000 on Sunday 8 February 2026 to more than 1.3 million the next day, and to more than 2.3 million on 11 February. The campaign then held a strict weekly cadence — heavy on weekdays, collapsing to near zero on Sundays — and reached its peak of 2.37 million messages on 26 February 2026. Elevated volumes persisted for about three months before dropping sharply after 15 May. The bulk of it was finance-themed spam: fake pre-approvals for credit lines and “bridge funding.”

One figure from the same research is worth more than the spike: Microsoft reports that over 99% of these messages were caught anyway — by sender, IP, URL and domain reputation, ML classification, brand-impersonation checks and authentication, not by noticing the invisible characters. The Unicode trick defeated one layer, and the other layers held. That is the shape of a working defence, and it is the argument this whole page is making.

The email angle is subtler than plain hiding, and it is aimed squarely at machine-learning classifiers. A word like funding is a clean, familiar unit to a model’s tokenizer. Slip an invisible U+E0020 into the middle and the tokenizer may split it into fun and ding, or emit rare tokens the model has never weighted — while the recipient still reads “funding.” The lure survives; the filter’s view of it does not. Want to see it? Load the spam lure in the Inspect & clean panel above and X-ray it, or take one word apart in What the tokenizer sees.

The property that makes tag characters useful for smuggling instructions into a model is the same property that makes them useful for hiding a keyword from a filter: they are invisible to a person and present to everything that processes text. The intent is inverted; the mechanism is not. — the mechanism in summary. Not a quotation; see REFERENCES for the sources.

Camouflaging trigger words isn’t new — spammers have leaned on zero-width characters, the no-break space U+00A0 and the soft hyphen U+00AD for decades to break literal-string matches (the NBSP & soft hyphen example in the Inspect panel is that older trick, unchanged). What’s new is the target. The bigger prize is no longer defeating a keyword search; it’s defeating the ML- and NLP-based models that now drive spam and phishing classification. Unless a filter renders the message to an image and runs OCR over it, the invisible tags can quietly change what the model “reads.”

You are not the only one watching for this

The Inspect panel above is a friendly version of checks that already ship in tools you probably use every day. Trojan Source is why most of them exist.

  • VS Code — Unicode highlighting. The editor flags invisible, ambiguous and non-basic-ASCII characters inline. It is on by default for ambiguous characters; the settings that govern it live under editor.unicodeHighlight.* (invisibleCharacters, ambiguousCharacters, nonBasicASCII). The closest productised sibling to this page’s detector.
  • GitHub — bidirectional-text warning. Diffs and blobs containing bidi control characters get a banner, added directly in response to Trojan Source.
  • The Rust compiler. A lint fires on bidi control codepoints inside string literals; other toolchains added equivalents. Boucher and Anderson’s 2021 Trojan Source paper (CVE-2021-42574 / 42694) is the common ancestor of all of these.
  • Your browser, on every address bar. The Punycode display rules and Unicode’s UTS #39 confusables data are the defence against the look-alike forge above — which is why that panel shows you the xn-- form the browser would fall back to.
  • Microsoft Defender for Office. The ASCII-smuggling signature behind the chart further up this page — and a working lesson in what a signature costs. Microsoft reports that its first version kept firing on legitimate mail, because the England, Scotland and Wales flag emoji are built out of tag characters; the three sequences had to be excluded before the signature was usable. A naive “any codepoint in U+E0000–E007F” rule is not ground truth. The Inspect panel implements the same exception, and the Flag emoji example above shows it working.

The pattern is worth noticing: every one of these defences is a rendering decision. None of them change the bytes. They make the invisible visible and let a human decide — which is the same move the Inspect panel makes.

What this page deliberately does not do

Everything here hides data in characters, which is why all of it can be detected by looking at characters. Three neighbouring fields do not have that weakness, and are worth knowing about precisely because this page cannot demonstrate them honestly:

  • Stego in a model’s token choices. Meteor (Kaptchuk et al.) and arithmetic-coding schemes hide bits in which word a language model picks, so the output is ordinary text with no anomalous codepoint to find. That is the real answer to the weakness listed below — and it needs a language model, so it cannot run in a single HTML file.
  • Watermarking generated text. SynthID-Text and green-list schemes (Kirchenbauer et al.) run the idea backwards: instead of smuggling something into text, they bias generation so the output carries a statistical signature. The Leak tracer above is the crude, character-based cousin.
  • Other media entirely. Least-significant-bit stego in images, and glyph-shape perturbation in fonts. Same goal, no Unicode involved.

Where this is weak

  • Hiding the content is strong; hiding its existence is not. The AES-GCM layer protects what the message says even if the text is intercepted. But the presence of a payload is trivial to detect — the Inspect panel above does it in one pass, and so do published filter signatures.
  • It does not survive everywhere. Many platforms and editors normalize or strip tag characters on paste or send, so the ghost layer can silently vanish in transit.
  • Not a substitute for a covert channel. Treat this as a teaching model of a known technique, not a way to move messages past a real adversary. Modern detectors already flag it — which is exactly why understanding it matters for defense.
